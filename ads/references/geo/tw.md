# Taiwan (TW)

GEO module for `ads`, loaded when `.agents/profile.md`'s `geo` list includes `TW`. Operational compliance pointers, not legal advice and not the full text of the law — every section below carries its own `last_verified` date and official source, but article-level exceptions, interpretive standards, and agency rulings keep moving; confirm the current version before launch. Treat current law, agency rulings, and the client's own legal counsel as authoritative. High-risk industries (medical, financial, pharmaceutical) — consult the client's compliance function before launch regardless of what's below.

## Three-layer test for medical/aesthetic advertising

The most common mistake in Taiwan medical advertising is collapsing it to "Medical Care Act §85 is an exhaustive list; anything outside it is banned." That error cuts both ways — it blocks treatment items and fees that are actually allowed, and it applies restrictions meant for paid ads to a clinic's own website content, which follows a different rule. The real test has three independent layers.

**Layer 1 — which item of §85(1) does this content fall under? (Item 6 is not empty.)**

§85(1) enumerates six items: ① the medical institution's name, license number, address, phone, and directions ② the physician's name, sex, education, experience, and license/specialist-certificate number ③ National Health Insurance or other non-commercial-insurance contracted-provider status ④ department and hours ⑤ opening, closing, suspension, resumption, relocation dates ⑥ **other content the central competent authority has announced as permitted.**

Item 6 is the one most often missed, and it is not a placeholder. The Ministry of Health and Welfare has issued an announcement under this item (No. 1031660048, 2014-01-24) that, subject to medical-ethics compliance, accurate information, patient guidance, and patient-safety conditions, permits listing **disease names, treatment items, examination/test items, medical devices and human-trial-completed medical techniques, and medical fees** — plus rules for international medical services and multi-provider listings. In other words: "we perform double-eyelid surgery," "ultrasound skin tightening, NT$X per session" — treatment items and fees — are themselves within the permitted scope, not outside-the-list violations.

Practical order: check items 1–5 → then check item 6's announced permitted-content list → only if neither applies is the content genuinely outside the permitted scope. The announcement's full text and any subsequent amendments are maintained at the Ministry of Health and Welfare's Medical Advertising Management section (<https://dep.mohw.gov.tw/DOMA/cp-2708-38120-106.html>); confirm the current version before taking on a client, don't work from this summary.

**Layer 2 — is the content a "medical advertisement" or the clinic's own "internet information"? (Different governing article.)**

§85(3): information a medical institution provides via the internet is, apart from the circumstances listed in §103(2) (false, exaggerated, distorted content, content offensive to public order, promoting illegal abortion, etc.), **not bound by §85(1)'s content scope and §86 does not apply** — it's instead governed by the "Regulations on Medical Institutions' Internet Information."

This layer matters specifically for digital media buying because it splits two things that get conflated: **the patient-education articles and case write-ups on a clinic's own website follow different rules than the ad creative bought on paid placements.** What a landing page (the clinic's own site) can say is broader than what a Google Ads headline or Meta creative can say. The failure runs both directions — pasting website-style copy straight into paid ad creative, or (overcorrecting) trimming the website itself down to just an address and phone number because "medical advertising is strict." Review ad creative and landing-page copy separately, and tell the client explicitly that the two pieces of copy sit under different articles.

**Layer 3 — does it trip one of §86's prohibited promotion methods? (Governs *how*, not *what*.)**

§86 prohibits: ① promoting under someone else's name ② promoting via the sale or gift of medical publications ③ promoting by publishing a "secret family remedy" or through public Q&A ④ excerpting medical-journal content for promotion ⑤ promoting via interview or news coverage ⑥ jointly or adjacently placing an ad with one that violates the preceding article ⑦ any other improper means.

"Guaranteed," "complete cure," "permanent," "never recurs," "#1 nationwide" and similar superlative/guarantee language can trip item 7 ("other improper means") even when describing a Layer-1-permitted treatment item, and separately risks §103(2)'s false/exaggerated-content prohibition. **This layer is independent of Layer 1** — a permitted item doesn't legalize any way of describing it: "ultrasound skin tightening" can run; "guaranteed results in one session with our ultrasound skin tightening" cannot.

**A citation to reject:** commentary (including AI-generated compliance summaries) sometimes claims Judicial Yuan Interpretation No. 744 "invalidated Medical Care Act §85's content restrictions." **This is a mismatch.** No. 744 (2017-01-06) addressed **cosmetics advertising's pre-broadcast review** — it struck down the pre-review requirements in the (former) Statute for Control of Cosmetic Hygiene §24(2) and §30(1) as unconstitutional (current cosmetics regulation has no pre-broadcast ad review as a result). It has nothing to do with Medical Care Act §85's permitted-content rules — §85 and §86 remain valid as of this file's verification date. Confirm which statute an interpretation actually addresses before citing it to back a medical-ad claim.

## PII and data minimization (refuse raw lists — a hard rule, not a suggestion)

This skill does not collect, store, or output any personally-identifiable audience list or contact information, and does not process a raw list a user pastes:

- **A user pasting a list fragment with names, emails, or phone numbers gets refused outright — don't summarize it, count rows, or respond to its content in any way.** Even reporting "this list has roughly X rows" requires having read and parsed the raw PII to produce that number — "only a summary came out" doesn't mean "no PII was processed." Correct response: tell the user this skill doesn't process raw lists, and ask them to aggregate or de-identify it themselves first (e.g., "about 5,000 rows, roughly 3,200 with verified email" — a statistic they computed, not one this skill derives from the raw file)
- Audience understanding stays at the **cohort level** — never any specific individual's identifying data
- Don't request unnecessary sensitive personal data (health status, financial hardship, political affiliation, sexual orientation, religious belief) for analysis, even at an aggregate level — there's no legitimate need for it here

### Uploading a list to an ad platform: Articles 19 and 20 are two separate gates, both must clear

**"We collected this list ourselves" doesn't mean "we can upload it for platform matching."** Uploading an existing list to Google or Meta for audience matching is a **use** under the Personal Data Protection Act (§20), not a **collection** (§19) — §19 only answers "could this data be collected in the first place," not "can it now be put to this new use." §20(1) requires that a non-government entity's use of personal data stay **within the necessary scope of the originally stated collection purpose**; using it outside that scope needs either separate consent or another statutory basis. Walk the user through two steps:

1. What purpose was disclosed when this list was originally collected — did it cover "providing this data to a third-party ad platform for audience matching"? (A disclosure that only said "send order notifications" clearly didn't.)
2. If it didn't — get separate consent, or find another statutory basis. **"We collected it ourselves as first-party data" is not on its own sufficient.**

### Special-category PII: this gate comes before Articles 19/20, and it fails closed

**There's a gate before Articles 19/20: does the list's very existence reveal a special category of the subject's personal data?** PDPA §6 imposes stricter rules on medical records, healthcare, genetic data, sexual life, health examinations, and criminal records — collection/processing/use is prohibited in principle, with narrow statutory exceptions.

The key is that **the list's selection criterion is itself information**: a list of "customers who bought diabetes test strips" — even if every field is a hashed email — reveals, once uploaded for platform matching, that "these people are connected to diabetes." **What looks like only an identifier actually conveys a health status.** Same pattern: infertility-treatment consultations, psychiatric appointment bookings, addiction-treatment program participants, registrants for a specific religious event.

Judgment sequence:

1. **Ask only about non-identifying metadata about the list, never its contents** (the raw-list refusal above still applies): "What criterion was this list filtered on?" "Does that criterion relate to medical history, health exams, sexual life, or criminal record?"
2. **Answer is "related" or "unsure" → fail closed.** Don't recommend uploading, and don't offer a workaround (e.g., "broaden it to all health-category customers" just dilutes the signal, it doesn't lift §6). Send the user to legal counsel to judge whether a §6 exception applies; this skill stops here
3. **Answer is "unrelated" → continue to the Articles 19/20 test above**
4. **Platform policy is an independent third gate**: Google/Meta have their own policies on sensitive categories (health status, financial hardship, etc.) — even after PDPA clears, the platform may still prohibit it. Check the platform's current policy page; don't cite this file for that answer

### Opt-out lists: suppress locally, never upload

PDPA §20(2)–(3) require a free opt-out mechanism at first marketing use and immediate cessation once someone opts out. There is only one correct technical implementation of this:

- **Correct: remove opted-out people locally, before generating the upload file.** The file sent to the platform should never contain any identifier (including a hashed one) for these people
- **Incorrect: uploading the opt-out list as a separate "excluded audience."** This is the intuitive move, but it does the opposite of what's intended — **to keep someone from being reached, it hands their identifier to the ad platform.** The person opted out of marketing use; building a platform audience from their data is itself a marketing-purpose use, enacting their wish in a way that violates it
- **The one exception**: if that person's identifier was **already uploaded previously** and is still in an existing platform audience, the correct move is to **remove them from that existing audience** using the platform's own removal function, not to build a new exclusion audience. Keep the local suppression list in place afterward so the next upload doesn't re-add them

One line: **an opt-out list is a local filter, not something to send the platform.**

**Platform terms are a separate gate**: Custom Audience / Customer Match / lookalike sources only accept genuine first-party lists — a purchased, scraped, or cold-outreach-collected list doesn't meet Google's/Meta's requirement that the advertiser collected the list themselves with disclosed intent to use it for audience matching. **This is a note for the user's own upload — this skill doesn't perform the upload or touch the list file's contents.**

| Common mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| Uploading a purchased, scraped, or cold-outreach list for Customer Match / Custom Audience | Doesn't meet the platform's first-party-list terms, and the collection itself may be unlawful |
| Uploading a legitimately-collected first-party list (disclosed only as "send order notifications") assuming "first-party means it's fine" | Treats §19 as if it were §20 — lawful collection doesn't make this new use lawful |
| Uploading a list filtered on a medical/health criterion, assuming hashed emails make it safe | The list's **selection criterion itself** reveals health status — falls under §6 special-category data, hashing doesn't resolve it |
| Uploading an opt-out list as an "excluded audience" | To avoid reaching this person, it hands their identifier to the platform — exactly the use they opted out of. Suppress locally instead, see above |

## LINE Official Account push-message boundary

Unlike cold email, a LINE OA's audience has already added the account as a friend — one-to-many push messages must follow LINE's own commercial-messaging rules (no excessive push frequency, content must fit the Official Account terms of use), or the account risks suspension.

## Applying this to RSA and other short, regulated-industry ad assets

`.agents/profile.md` or a positioning document showing a Taiwan medical facility, aesthetic clinic, pharmaceutical, or supplement business → read the six-industry table above before applying the checks below.

**Checked object is every piece of text that displays** — not only headlines and descriptions. Sitelink titles and descriptions, callout values, structured-snippet values, and display paths all get the same check; a 25-character callout isn't a looser bar than a 30-character headline, both are advertising content in the law's eyes.

- **Medical / aesthetic** (Medical Care Act §§85, 86): an RSA headline or description is **paid advertising content** — it falls under §85(1)'s permitted-content scope and does **not** get §85(3)'s internet-information exception (a clinic's own website copy can say more than an RSA can; don't cut a website paragraph straight into a headline). Check both, independently:
  - **① Which item of §85(1) does this headline/description fall under?** Item 6 is not empty — the MOHW announcement permits **disease names, treatment items, examination/test items, medical devices and human-trial-completed techniques, and medical fees**. So "ultrasound skin tightening," "myopia laser consultation," "initial exam NT$X" have a basis and don't need to be flattened into neutral language. What's genuinely out of scope: **efficacy claims, before/after comparisons, testimonials** — none of §85(1)'s items permit these
  - **② Does it trip §86's prohibited promotion methods?** "Guaranteed," "complete cure," "permanent," "never recurs," "#1 nationwide" and similar superlative/guarantee language falls under §86 item 7 ("other improper means") and risks §103(2)'s false/exaggerated prohibition — never use these in a headline or description, regardless of item 1's outcome. **This check is independent of check ①** — a permitted item doesn't legalize any way of describing it
  - Full three-layer test, including the No. 744 citation pitfall, above
- **Supplements / functional foods** (Food Safety and Sanitation Act §28): no medical-efficacy terms ("treat," "cure," "heal," "prevent/improve [disease]"); no "regulates physiological function" claim without an approved Health Food mark
- **Pharmaceuticals** (Pharmaceutical Affairs Act §66): ad copy (including RSA headlines/descriptions) is part of the approved broadcast content — it must match the client's already-approved version; don't self-modify approved copy to produce a new RSA variant

**A blocked efficacy-claim headline** substitutes a neutral term instead — "consultation," "evaluation," "book an assessment," "learn more." This is a **substitute, not a blanket downgrade** — announced permitted treatment items, exam items, and fees can still be stated plainly. Add a geographic modifier ("Taipei," "Xinyi District") when the product explicitly targets a specific city or district.

The above is operational guidance, not legal advice or the complete text of the law — Taiwan's Medical Care Act, Pharmaceutical Affairs Act, and Food Safety and Sanitation Act carry more complete rules and exceptions (including sub-regulation determination standards) than fit here. Have the client's legal or compliance contact review copy before launch, especially after a regulation changes.
